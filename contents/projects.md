#### Kinematic Signatures in Open Clusters

Open clusters are groups of stars that were born together from the same gas cloud. Because they share the same origin, they are great places to learn how groups of stars form, change, and eventually fall apart. The way stars move inside a cluster tells us a lot about its past. A cluster might be spinning, growing, shrinking, or being stretched, and each of these motions leaves a clue about how it was born and what has happened to it since.

Over time, the Milky Way's gravity slowly pulls stars out of the cluster. These escaped stars form long trails called tidal tails. The tails keep a record of the cluster's history and also show how our Galaxy shapes the clusters within it. Thanks to the Gaia space telescope, we can now measure the motions of these stars in great detail for the first time.

#### Membership of Open Clusters and Their Tidal Tails

Before we can study a cluster, we need to know which stars actually belong to it. This sounds simple, but it is not. Cluster stars are mixed with many unrelated stars in the same part of the sky, and even a few wrong stars can give us the wrong age, distance, or motion for the cluster.

This problem is even harder for tidal tails. Tail stars are few and spread out over a large area, so they easily blend in with the stars around them. Newly found clusters are also tricky because we know little about them yet. Better ways to find cluster and tail members help us make the most of Gaia data and understand how clusters slowly dissolve into the Milky Way.

#### INAGLENS: Strong Gravitational Lens Modelling

A massive galaxy can bend light like a lens. When it lines up almost perfectly with a more distant galaxy behind it, the light from the far galaxy gets stretched into arcs, rings, or several copies. These rare cosmic lenses let us weigh the front galaxy, including its invisible dark matter, and they also magnify the faraway galaxy so we can see it more clearly.

New sky surveys, like those from the Subaru Telescope, are finding these lenses in large numbers. By studying many of them together, we can learn how mass, both visible and dark, is spread inside galaxies and how this has changed over the history of the universe.
